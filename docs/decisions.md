# Decisions

Short architecture decision records, newest last.

## 1. A pure, immutable core shared by the app and the demo

**Context.** The brief was an Electron app; later we wanted a static web demo and full test coverage.
**Decision.** All behaviour lives in `src/core` as pure functions over immutable state. The Electron
renderer and the demo are thin shells around the same `Stage`.
**Consequences.** The demo is the real engine, not a re-implementation. Tests need no Electron or DOM.

## 2. Seeded randomness only

**Decision.** `Math.random` is banned by ESLint; randomness comes from `core/rng.ts` with the seed
threaded through state. **Consequences.** Simulations, screenshots and tests are reproducible.

## 3. tsc only for the app build; vitest for tests

The original brief said no extra transpilers, so the app compiles with `tsc` alone (CommonJS for main
and preload, ES modules for the web code). Vitest and Playwright are test tooling.

## 4. `electron-store` 8, not 9

v9 is ESM-only and breaks a CommonJS main process.

## 5. Login items via Electron, not raw registry or AppleScript

`app.setLoginItemSettings` writes the Windows `Run` key and registers the macOS login item; only Linux
needs a hand-written `.desktop` file.

## 6. Redistributable art only in the repo and demo

The classic eSheep artwork has no clear licence. It can be fetched for personal use into a
git-ignored folder that is excluded from installers and never reaches the demo. The repo ships
`woolly` and `builtin`, generated by a script.

## 7. Window-edge rules learned the hard way

- A blocked sheep slides right up to the edge (binary search) instead of stopping a step short;
  otherwise wall and ceiling contexts such as climbing never fire.
- "Entered the screen" means the lower body is on a display. A sheep standing on a high window may
  have its head above the screen; that must not block walking.
- Before it has entered, a sheep at floor height counts as on the floor even if it is beside the
  display, so a sheep spawning in from the side is not dropped by gravity.

## 8. The README is generated

Pills and screenshots are rewritten by `npm run readme` in CI from the real reports, and committed by
a bot with `[skip ci]`. A pure, unit-tested module (`src/tools/metrics.ts`) does the rendering.

## 9. Electron 45 and electron-builder 26, not the briefed `^28`

The original brief pinned `electron ^28`. `npm audit` flags Electron 28 (long out of support) and
electron-builder 24's `tar`/`app-builder-lib` at high or critical severity, and CI gates on high. We
moved to Electron 45, electron-builder 26 and fast-xml-parser 5. The real-Electron Playwright suite
passed unchanged, which is the point of having it. Install-script approvals in `package.json` are
name-based so a dependency bump never silently skips the Electron binary download.

## 10. Native-size sprites, and hopping onto windows

Classic packs are drawn at native pixels (`SCALE = 1`). The original eSheep art is 40 px per tile;
blowing it up to 2x looked wrong. The 64 px generated pack is centred in the 128 px window.

The original pack never climbs windows (only #1 has a `windowSide` border, and `jump` is a hop on
the spot), so the engine adds a `hop`: on meeting the side of a window it can reach, or while
approaching a ledge above head height, a sheep plays `jump` and follows an arc onto the window top.
Reach is capped at 16 sprite heights and the target must keep the sprite on a display.

## 11. A full original sprite pack for the demo

The Pages demo ships `woolly`: 118 original 40 px poses (`scripts/classic/art.mjs`, drawn
procedurally, MIT) driving all 56 classic animations: turning, sleeping, weeing, bathing, climbing,
falling, tossing and more. Sequencing (timings, transitions, spawns) follows the MIT-licensed
desktopPet format and is recorded in `scripts/classic/structure.json` with frames named by pose,
so no original artwork is involved. The demo has an Animation picker to trigger any of them.
`npm run assets` regenerates the sheet; the original eSheep art remains local-only.

## 12. Faster, livelier, and a flock

The generated pack runs at a faster tempo (every interval halved; the walking strides tripled, as
the classic walk is only ~10 px/s) and its transition weights are tuned so climbing, ceiling walks,
grazing, flowers, boing and dozing turn up in minutes rather than hours (`scripts/classic/pack.mjs`).
The engine treats the end of a window as an edge, so `look_down`, `jump_down` and the window-fall
animations play instead of the sheep silently dropping off.

Several sheep: the demo keeps one canvas and `Stage` per sheep; the Electron app opens one
transparent window per sheep (renderers are independent, IPC replies go to the sender's window),
with `count` (1-12) in prefs, Add/Remove in the tray and a Sheep field in Settings.

## 13. Tuning is applied at load, to every classic pack

The faster tempo and tuned weights live in `src/main/tune.ts` (pure, unit-tested) and are applied by
`loadPack` and the demo build, not baked into any pack. The generated `woolly` pack therefore stores
the classic sequencing untouched, and the local-only `original` art gets the same liveliness.
`npm run demo:original` (`build-demo.mjs --with-original`) adds the original art to a local demo
build behind a `packs/index.json` listing; CI never passes the flag, so it cannot be published.

## 14. The original eSheep art ships, and is the default

The owner has confirmed they hold a licence for the original eSheep artwork, so it is committed
(`assets/packs/original/`, with a NOTICE), bundled in installers, and the demo's default sheep. The
generated `woolly` pack stays as an MIT-licensed alternative. This reverses the earlier "local-only"
stance (ADR 11 and 13); the `--with-original` build flag and `demo:original` script are gone.

## 15. Sheep leap over each other

`World.others` carries the other sheep's sprite rectangles. A walking sheep that closes on one at
the same floor level (within a quarter sprite, not already mostly overlapping) hops over it half the
time per tick, reusing the window `hop` with a higher arc (1.3 sprites) and landing just past the
far side, only if that spot has floor under it. Sleeping or grazing sheep get jumped over, and two
sheep walking at each other pass one another. In the app each renderer polls the main process
(`get-other-sheep`, window bounds of the other windows, every 50 ms) and insets them to sprite
rectangles; in the demo the flock shares rectangles directly.

## 16. Greeting, macOS windows and DPI

- **Greeting:** when a sheep closes on another at the same level it first rolls for a greeting (15%:
  the pack's `sync` animation, which the classic data defines but never reaches) before leaping.
  `petSide` transitions (also in the classic data, previously ignored) now apply while another sheep
  stands right beside it.
- **macOS:** `node-window-manager` already supports macOS. `createWindowSource` now loads it there,
  asks for the Accessibility permission (falling back to screen edges if refused) and keeps untitled
  windows, since titles need Screen Recording permission.
- **DPI:** the library divides Windows bounds by the window's own monitor scale factor, which is only
  right on one monitor. We undo that (`physicalBounds`) and convert the physical rectangle with
  Electron's `screen.screenToDipRect`, which is correct per display on mixed-DPI desktops.
  None of macOS or DPI has been run on real hardware; the logic is unit-tested with fakes.

## 17. Windows sized to the sprite

Each sheep window used to be a fixed 128 px square around a 40 px sprite, which left the window
hanging 44 px below the display when the sheep stood on the floor (a problem on platforms that clamp
windows to the screen, such as macOS) and made twelve sheep twelve large transparent windows. A
classic sheep's window is now the sprite plus a 6 px margin (`PAD`); the stage sizes its own canvas
and reports `windowSize()`, and the renderer asks the main process to resize the window
(`set-window-size`, lifting `resizable` around the call because Linux ignores `setSize` otherwise).
The bounce sheep keeps its fixed 128 px square. Windows start at 52 px, so the usual case needs no
resize. Other sheep's rectangles are derived from their window bounds, centred.

## 18. High resolution: device-resolution canvas and a Size setting

The canvas used to be one backing pixel per CSS pixel, so on a display with a device pixel ratio of
2 or more the browser upscaled it with smoothing and the pixel art went soft. The stage now renders
into a canvas of `windowSize x pixelRatio` pixels (CSS size unchanged), draws under a
`setTransform(ratio)` with smoothing off, and maps hit-tests through the ratio. The renderer and demo
check `devicePixelRatio` every frame, so a window dragged to a monitor of a different density
re-renders at the right resolution (`setPixelRatio`). A `scale` preference (1 to 4, default 1) sets the
engine's `Config.scale`, which already multiplies both the sprite size and the walking strides, so a
bigger sheep also moves proportionally. Changing it, like changing pack, reloads the sheep windows.
The art itself is still the 40 px sheet; a higher-resolution sheet would need new artwork.

## 19. Kick-about (demo prototype)

A ball is its own pure body (`src/core/ball.ts`): ballistic bounce in the air, rolling with
exponential friction on floors and window tops, and it rolls off window edges. The engine takes
`World.ball`; a sheep on a floor whose current animation is a locomotion one (it won't wake from a
sleep for it) notices a ball on its level within 14 sprites, turns and runs at it, and when within a
quarter sprite emits a `Kick` (velocity, scaled by the sprite scale) with a 1.1 s cooldown. The sheep
never owns the ball: `Update.kicks` is an event the caller applies, so the same engine can later run
against a ball held in the Electron main process. `tunePack` synthesises a `kick` from the pack's
`run_end` skid when a pack has none. The demo owns the ball (a DOM circle), lets you grab and throw it
with the same pointer code as the sheep, and exposes `__demo.kickAbout/ball/kicks` for tests.

Bug found on the way: a body outside every display (a ball dragged past the edge, or a sheep thrown
from off-screen) never reflected or landed and fell forever. `stepBallistic` now moves such a body
into the nearest display first (`clampIntoAreas`), and the held ball is clamped to the playfield.

Not done: the Electron app. There each sheep is its own window and renderer, so a shared ball needs
the main-process simulation discussed in the design notes (ball as its own transparent window,
opt-in, time-limited). Scoring and teams are also not built.

## 20. Kick-about in the app: the ball lives in the main process

Each sheep is its own window and renderer, so the ball cannot live in any one of them. The main
process owns it (`src/main/kickabout.ts`, a small controller with injected world, size and areas so
it is unit-tested without Electron), steps it every 16 ms with the same pure `core/ball` physics the
demo uses, and moves a dedicated ball window (`ball.html`, a 14 px square times the sprite scale,
click-through is left off because the window _is_ the ball). Sheep renderers poll `get-ball` with
the other-sheep poll (50 ms) and extrapolate the ball by its velocity between polls (capped at
100 ms); a kick is sent back as `ball-kick`. The ball window reports pointer events in screen
coordinates (`ball-grab/move/release`), so you can carry and throw it with the shared pointer code.

Guard rails, since a ball bouncing over your work gets old fast: it is opt-in (tray item), ends by
itself after two minutes, is stopped by changing the pack or size, and a held ball cannot be dragged
off the displays. `get-windows` and `get-work-areas` are now memoised for 150 ms, which also removes
the per-sheep native enumeration cost flagged earlier (`node-window-manager` was called per renderer
per poll). The app's test seam gained `startKickAbout/stopKickAbout/kickAbout/placeBall`.
Verified in real Electron under Xvfb; not on a real Windows or macOS desktop.

## 21. Tag and stacking

**Stacking.** The backs of the other sheep (their rectangles, 20% down from the top) are floors in
the engine, alongside windows: a falling sheep lands on whoever is below, standing on a back counts
as standing on a window (so window-only transitions apply), and a sheep that walks into another
rolls to hop onto its back (15% of the meeting roll; 15% greet, 35% leap over). The landing spot
must be free, which caps a stack at one passenger per sheep per level but still allows towers. A
passenger falls when the carrier walks off. Measured in the demo (six sheep, three simulated
minutes): about 10% of the time someone is riding, 106 landings.

**Tag.** `core/tag.ts` is a pure, id-based game: one sheep is "it", it chases the nearest sheep on
its level, and touching one (same level, no gap) swaps the role; the new "it" counts for 1.5 s so the
old one can run. `viewFor` tells each sheep its role and target; the engine gets this as `World.tag`.
Runners flee "it" and hop over it if cornered against the screen edge; while a game is on, ordinary
greeting and leaping are switched off so they don't undermine it. In the demo the flock runs the
game directly and "it" gets a red glow. In the app the main process owns the game
(`main/tagplay.ts`), derives sprite rectangles from the sheep windows' bounds (minus the shared
`WINDOW_PAD`), swaps on contact every 50 ms, and each sheep window polls `get-tag` for its own view
and glows red when it is "it". It is unavailable for the bounce sheep, ends when the pack or size
changes, and starting it adds a second sheep if there is only one.

Not done: tag between sheep on different levels. (Score and teams came later: ADR 28.)

## 22. Releases: tag-triggered, with a self-tested Windows build

Pushing a `v*` tag runs the Package workflow on Windows, macOS and Linux, and a final `release` job
publishes the installers plus `SHA256SUMS.txt` as a GitHub Release (`gh release create`, no third-party
action). The tag must equal `v<package.json version>`; the Windows installer and portable exe are
mandatory for the release to be published. File names are fixed in `electron-builder.yml`
(`eSheep-Setup-<version>-x64.exe`, `eSheep-Portable-<version>-x64.exe`, ...) so download links are stable.

The Windows job also **starts the packaged exe** with `ESHEEP_SELFTEST=<file>`: the app opens its sheep
window, waits for the canvas, writes a JSON report (platform, version, whether `node-window-manager`
loaded, sheep windows, packs, `rendered`), and exits. The job fails unless it rendered and the native
module is available, so a release cannot ship a build that does not start or has lost its native
window support. The builds are unsigned (no certificate), so SmartScreen warns; the release notes
and README say how to proceed. This verifies startup on a real Windows runner, not hand-use.

## 23. Sheep are rescued when the displays change under them

Resizing the browser window (demo) or changing resolution / unplugging a monitor (app) moves the
walls and floors. A sheep that had been standing at the old right or bottom edge was then off every
display: stuck against a wall that moved, or falling past a floor that was now above it
(`y` in the thousands in the reproduction). `update` now starts with `rescue`: an entered sheep
whose lower body is on no display is put on the nearest one (`clampIntoAreas`, the same helper the
thrown-body physics and the held ball use), and the normal rules take over. Sheep that have not yet
walked in, and sheep legitimately standing on a window near the top with their head off-screen, are
untouched.

Releasing, as done for v0.1.0 (and two pitfalls): bump `version` in `package.json`, commit, then
`git tag -a vX.Y.Z <commit> && git push origin vX.Y.Z`. **Tag a commit whose message does not contain
`[skip ci]`**: GitHub suppresses push-triggered workflows for it, including tag pushes, and the README
bot's commits all carry that marker (tag the code commit, not the bot's commit after it). The release
job has no checkout, so `gh` needs `GH_REPO`. A run uses the workflow file as it was at the tag, so a
workflow fix means moving the tag (safe only while no release exists for it).

## 24. Sheep are solid to each other

Measured first: with eight sheep and the windows visible, two sheep interpenetrated by a full sprite in
about 27% of frames (about 60% during tag), because meeting another sheep only _sometimes_ triggered a
hop, greeting or climb and otherwise they walked straight through. Four layers fix it:

1. **Hard block** in `move()`: a sheep may not walk (or fall) _into_ an overlap (touching is fine;
   standing on a back, 20% down, is fine). It stops beside the other and the pack's border response
   turns it round. A sheep already overlapping may still walk out.
2. **Always decide**: meeting resolves into greet, climb, leap, or the block turning it away.
3. **Only one hops**: if both sheep leapt over each other at once they passed through each other in
   mid-air (found by a long two-sheep test). `mayHop` decides from a symmetric function of the pair's
   positions, so both sheep agree without talking, and it varies from meeting to meeting. Takeoff also
   applies its first frame immediately so the other sheep sees it airborne. A sheep steering for or
   from "it" in tag leaves the other to the game.
4. **Push-out and flight avoidance**: `separate()` slides overlapping sheep apart (half the remaining
   depth, at least 6 px, per update), `hopStep` drifts past anyone who moved under the flight path or
   into the landing spot, and `move()` slides a sheep that would fall into another's side out sideways.

Result in the demo: about 0.1% of frames (25-28% before; tag 0.1-0.6%, was 58-66%), and tag got
slightly faster (70-101 tags per ten minutes, was 57-71) because "it" now stops beside its target. In
the real app, where each sheep sees the others up to 50 ms late, 0.3% of samples overlap with a worst
case of 7-10 px. The remainder are the ~50 ms when a hopping sheep touches down where another has just
stepped and the standing one slides clear. Tests: unit (in-turn and stale-position models over six
seeds and 40 simulated seconds) and real-browser and real-Electron e2e with measured ceilings.

## 25. Keeping the Electron e2e honest on a slow CI runner

Cutting v0.1.1 took four CI rounds, all about the tests, none about the app. What was learned:

- **Never evaluate on a window you just told to reload.** Changing the pack or size reloads the sheep
  window; a following `page.evaluate` on it dies with "Execution context was destroyed". Read anything
  you need (like the work-area bottom) from the main process (`app.evaluate`), which does not reload.
- **Sample against the wall clock, inside the main process.** The first app overlap test did 300
  round trips across six windows and blew the 120 s limit on a slow runner; it now runs one
  `app.evaluate` loop for 25 s with four windows.
- **Tear down defensively.** `app.close()` was seen to hang for minutes after a test that had passed,
  and the Playwright worker-teardown timeout then fails the whole job even when the retry passes.
  `afterEach` now races `close()` against 15 s, then kills the process and logs that it did.
- **Reproduce before changing.** Squeezing the suite onto one or two cores with busy loops
  (`taskset -c 0,1` plus background `while :; do :; done`) reproduced the reload race immediately and
  then proved the fixes (two full runs, `--retries=0`).
- Packaging output (`release/`) is excluded from markdown lint, which the pre-commit hook had picked up.
- New sheep windows are spread out at creation instead of all starting at screen centre.

## 26. The desktop app defaults to the original art (and old installs are migrated)

0.1.0 and 0.1.1 shipped with `builtin` as the default pack: the generated, simple bouncing sheep in a
fixed 128 px window. That is three times the size of the 40 px classic sprite and not the art the demo,
the README or the screenshots show, so a fresh install of the desktop app looked wrong and "huge".
Verified by launching a fresh-userData build: `pack: builtin`, window 128x128. Because electron-store
_writes its defaults to `config.json` on first run_, changing `DEFAULT_PREFS` alone would not have
helped anyone who already installed it, so `src/main/prefs.ts` adds a one-time migration keyed on a
`version` field: below version 2, a stored `builtin` becomes `original` (or `woolly` if that is
missing). `version` is deliberately not one of the store's defaults, otherwise electron-store would
fill it in for an existing install and the migration would never run. A user who picks the bouncing
sheep afterwards keeps it. The Settings list now shows friendly names ("Original eSheep", "Woolly
(generated)", "Bouncing sheep (simple)"). The e2e suite seeds the old bouncing-sheep preferences for the
tests that need that sheep, and has four new tests for fresh installs, the upgrade, and a deliberate
choice surviving it.

## 27. Automatic releases

Releasing used to be: bump the version, push, wait for CI, then tag by hand (ADR 22). It is now: bump
the version and push (`npm run release -- patch|minor|major`), and a workflow does the rest.

`release.yml` runs when the **CI** workflow completes on a push to main (`workflow_run`; never for pull
requests). Its `check` job reads `package.json`, requires a plain `x.y.z`, requires CI to have passed on
that exact commit (checked through the API, which also covers a manual run), and asks whether
`gh release view vX.Y.Z` exists. Only if it does not does the `release` job call `package.yml` as a
reusable workflow (`workflow_call`) with the commit and the tag.

**Why not just push a tag from a workflow?** Events caused by the built-in `GITHUB_TOKEN` (including a tag
push) do not start other workflows, so an auto-tagger would appear to work and release nothing. Calling
the reusable workflow avoids needing a personal access token. `package.yml` now has a small `meta` job that
works out the commit and tag whichever way it was started (called, tag pushed, or run by hand), refuses a
tag that is not `vX.Y.Z` (inputs reach the shell through environment variables), and the final
`gh release create --target <sha>` creates the tag itself at the built commit. This also removes ADR 22's
`[skip ci]` pitfall: the commit being built is passed explicitly, not inferred from a tag. A concurrency
group per tag stops two near-simultaneous CI passes releasing the same version twice.

`scripts/release.mts` is only git plumbing around `src/tools/release.mts` (pure and unit-tested): it
refuses unless you are on a clean, up-to-date `main` with CI green on HEAD, bumps, commits and pushes.
The decision to release is still a human one (bump the version); everything after it is automatic.
Releasing on every merge, or deriving the version from conventional commits, would be a further step.

## 28. Soccer: teams, goals and a score

`core/soccer.ts` is a pure, id-based match like tag. Sheep alternate between red and blue by id order
(`teamOf`), so the sides stay balanced as sheep come and go; red attacks right and blue attacks left.
The goals are mouths on the floor at the far left and far right of the display area (2 ball-widths
deep, 9 tall), so they follow multi-monitor layouts. A ball that overlaps a mouth scores for the other
team, then the match pauses for a 2.5 s kick-off and the ball is put back in the middle; goals are
ignored during the pause, so one shot scores once. First to 5 wins; the result stays up for 8 s and
the match ends. A match is cut off after 5 minutes whatever happens, like every opt-in mode.

**Playing the right way round.** The engine gets `World.team = { dir }`. A sheep only kicks when it is
on the correct side of the ball (the ball is in the direction it attacks); from the wrong side it keeps
running, past the ball, then turns and kicks. That needs no passing or positional play and still looks
like a match. Measured in the demo over six seeds: the first goal comes after 4 to 18 simulated
seconds and a match finishes in 1 to 5 minutes.

**Demo and app.** The demo runs the match directly (Soccer button, DOM goals and scoreboard, teams
glow red or blue). In the app the main process owns it (`main/soccerplay.ts` on top of the shared
kick-about ball, so one ball and one set of physics) and shows it with three click-through overlay
windows: two goals and a scoreboard (`app/overlay.html`). Each sheep window polls `get-team` for its
team and glows in its colour. It is unavailable for the bounce sheep, tops the sheep up to four (two a
side), and starting tag or stopping the ball ends it. `kickAbout.start` gained a time limit so a match
can outlast a plain kick-about.

Not done: passing, positions or a goalkeeper, a throw-in when the ball leaves play, and picking the
teams yourself.

## 28. Warped sheep on Windows, and a proper tray

**The sheep stretched across the screen (reported with a screenshot from Windows).** The picture was a
thin strip as wide as the screen with the sheep smeared along it: the sheep's _window_ had grown huge
and the canvas was CSS-stretched (`100vw`/`100vh`) to fill it. The likely cause (inferred; there is no
Windows machine here to reproduce on) is a known Electron-on-Windows quirk: with display scaling other
than 100%, calling `setPosition` on a frameless transparent window rounds DIPs to physical pixels each
time and the _size_ creeps, and we called it for every sheep on every frame. Three layers, so no single
one has to be right: `move-window` now uses `setBounds` with the window's intended size (kept per window
in `sheepSizes`), re-asserting the whole rectangle every move so it cannot drift; the canvas has a fixed
pixel size and is never stretched; and the renderer checks `innerWidth/innerHeight` twice a second and asks
the main process to restore the exact size if the OS has changed it (which also handles a dragged edge).
`set-window-size` now compares width _and_ height and lifts `resizable` around the call (Linux ignores
resizing otherwise). Tests force a window to 1800x30 from outside and assert it heals to 52x52, that the
canvas stays 52 CSS px even while the window is wrong, and that the size never changes while walking. The
actual Windows DPI creep cannot be reproduced on Linux.

**The tray.** The icon was a generated white blob, resized to 16 px with `.resize()`, which also throws
away any high-DPI variants. It is now the front-facing sheep (tile 10) from the original sheet, made by
`npm run icons` (`scripts/make-icons.mjs`, which drives Chromium to cut the tile and key the magenta):
`tray.png` 16 px plus `tray@1.25x/1.5x/2x/3x.png` for every Windows DPI step, which `createFromPath`
picks up when the image is used as loaded; `assets/icon.png` for the settings window, and
`build/icon.png` (512 px) which electron-builder turns into the `.ico`/`.icns`/Linux icon. The menu is a
pure function, `buildTrayTemplate(state, actions)` in `src/main/trayMenu.ts`, so every label, tick and
enabled state is unit-tested without Electron: Pause/Resume; **Sheep** (add, remove, a radio list of
counts); **Appearance** (pack, size, speed radios); **Games**; checkboxes for sound and start-at-login
(worded per platform, hidden in a development run); Settings; Downloads and updates; Quit. Choices go
through the same `applyPrefs` as the Settings window, so they behave identically and persist. Start at
login used to be forced on at every launch; it is now a preference (on at first run, then the user's
choice). Windows shows nothing on a plain left-click and a menu set with `setContextMenu` goes stale, so
there the tray pops up a freshly built menu for both buttons; other platforms replace the menu whenever
something changes. Right-clicking a sheep opens the same menu with **Remove this sheep** on top (only the
primary button grabs a sheep now). The packaged-app self-test also reports the tray icon (size, scale
factors) and menu size, and the Windows CI job fails unless they are right.

## 29. Stuck sheep, a stuck ball, and sheep off the screen

Three reports, all measured before fixing (a headless run of the demo, five to eight sheep, windows
visible, four simulated minutes per seed):

**Sheep that walk but do not move.** Free play, kick-about and soccer showed none, but _tag_ showed up to
8.3 s of it. A runner fleeing "it" (asleep) ran into a sheep that was eating against the screen edge; the
overlap block (ADR 24) stopped it entering, but nothing made it turn, so it stayed in a `run` animation
going nowhere until "it" woke. The same shape would pin a sheep chasing an unreachable ball, or one
walking into an invisible "window" on a real desktop (Windows reports cloaked UWP windows as visible).
Rather than another special case there is now a **stuck watchdog** in the engine: a sheep in a locomotion
animation that makes no progress for 1.5 s turns round and walks away, with steering (ball chase, tag)
switched off for 2.5 s so it does not turn straight back in; if it is wedged again within that time it is
slid to the nearest free spot on its own floor (`nearestFreeX`). Result: stuck time in tag went from up
to 8.3 s to zero, with overlap and tag throughput unchanged.

**The soccer ball stuck on the screen.** Reproduced: with windows visible the ball sat motionless for 25-31
s, always on top of a window (a perch nobody can reach), and in the demo `Stop soccer` left the ball behind
altogether (`stopMatch` never removed the ball it had started). Fixes: kick-offs and re-drops use
`dropSpot`, the clear column nearest the middle with no window in it, so the ball falls to the floor; a
ball that lies still for 6 s (`REST_RESET_MS`) is dropped in again, in both the app's controller and the
demo; during a match sheep know where the ball is however far away (the 14-sprite notice range only applies
outside a match); a held ball nobody has moved for 8 s is let go (a lost mouse-up used to freeze it, and the
sheep cannot kick a held ball); the ball window only reacts to the primary button and lets go on a lost
pointer capture or on blur; and the demo removes a ball the match brought, but keeps one you already had.
Result: the ball is never perched off the floor, and matches finish roughly twice as fast.

**Sheep walking off the screen.** Not reproducible in the demo (0% of frames outside the monitor in free
play, tag, kick-about and soccer), so this is defence in depth rather than a located cause. Spawns that
started beyond the right edge (`screenW+10`, the classic walk-in) now start just inside it; and the main
process, which places the windows, never puts a sheep with its body off every display
(`clampWindow`, using each window's margin), whatever the renderer asks for, tested by asking for positions
at +/-99999 in every direction. Ceilings and heads poking above a display are still allowed. If it still
happens the next step is to learn which edge, and whether it is a multi-monitor or high-DPI setup.
